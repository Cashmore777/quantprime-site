### SLIDE 3: The Myths

Let's address four common myths that hold traders back.

Myth one: "Markets are random."

This is incorrect. Financial markets operate on underlying mechanics that can be understood and anticipated. The traders who claim randomness simply haven't developed the skills to recognise the patterns. Throughout this program, you'll learn to identify the algorithmic flow that drives price movement.

Myth two: "Trading is too difficult."

Trading isn't inherently difficult. It's unfamiliar. What makes it feel hard is approaching it without structure, without a clear methodology, and without proper guidance. The process itself is learnable. The challenge is psychological, not intellectual.

Myth three: "One strategy is better than another."

All viable strategies work within the same market structure. What matters isn't which strategy you choose, but how refined that strategy becomes through practice and iteration. A simple approach, mastered completely, will outperform a complex approach executed poorly.

Myth four: "You can get rich quickly."

Trading is based on percentages of capital. If you risk one percent of a one hundred pound account, you're working with one pound. Wealth is built through compounding over time, not through overnight windfalls. Expect gradual, sustainable growth. Anything else is gambling, not trading.

### SLIDE 6: Understanding Risk

Risk management is the foundation of longevity in trading.

Your lot size determines how much capital is at stake on each trade. This should be calculated based on a fixed percentage of your account, not on emotion or recent results.

Here's a general framework:

Zero point two five to zero point five percent risk is considered conservative. This is ideal for building consistency over long periods.

Zero point five to one point five percent is moderate risk. This is appropriate for most developing traders.

One point five to three percent is elevated risk. This should only be used by experienced traders with proven track records.

Above three percent is aggressive. This accelerates both gains and losses significantly.

Choose a risk level and commit to it. Do not increase risk because you're feeling confident, and do not decrease it because you're feeling fearful. Consistency in risk management is what allows edge to compound over time.

### SLIDE 7: Measuring the Market

A pip is the standard unit of measurement in forex trading.

For most currency pairs, you're looking at the fourth decimal place. For Japanese yen pairs, it's the second decimal place. This is how distance is measured on a chart - how far price has moved from one point to another.

When you place a trade, your stop loss distance in pips, combined with your risk percentage, determines your position size. This is why accurate measurement matters. A miscalculation here throws off your entire risk profile.

TradingView has built-in tools that display pip distance automatically. Use them. Don't calculate manually when you don't have to.

### SLIDE 8: Trade Management

Once a trade is live, you have decisions to make.

The simplest approach is to hold until your full target is reached or your stop loss is hit. No intervention. No adjustment. This removes emotion from the equation entirely.

An alternative is to take partial profits along the way. You might close half the position at fifty percent of your target, then let the remainder run. If you do this, move your stop loss to breakeven on the remaining portion. This locks in some profit while leaving room for the full move.

Neither approach is superior. What matters is choosing one method and applying it consistently. Switching between styles based on how you feel in the moment is a recipe for inconsistency.

### SLIDE 9: Finding Your Style

Trading style should match your personality and lifestyle.

Are you someone who watches charts actively? Or do you prefer to set positions and check back later?

Active traders might prefer taking profits manually as levels are reached. Passive traders might benefit from set-and-forget execution with predefined targets.

There's no correct answer. But there is a wrong approach: changing your style based on emotion. If fear is making you close trades early, the solution isn't to keep doing it. The solution is to implement a rule that prevents it.

Your trading plan should be designed to protect you from yourself during moments of poor judgment.
