What the Filter Removed

Almost any filter you add will raise your win rate. That is nearly automatic and it is close to meaningless. The question that decides whether a filter helps is one that standard backtest output never answers: what was the expectancy of the trades it deleted?

The illusion of improvement

Add a filter to a strategy — trade only above the 200 EMA, only in London hours, only when RSI agrees — and run the backtest again. The win rate goes up. It almost always goes up, because a filter reduces the trade count, and reducing the trade count with any criterion correlated with outcome will improve the ratio of the survivors.

What frequently does not go up is total profit. And traders reliably choose the filter anyway, because win rate is the number displayed most prominently, is easiest to reason about, and feels like skill.

A filter that raises win rate and lowers net profit has not made you more selective. It has made you more expensive.

The mechanism is simple once stated. Filters do not remove losers. They remove trades, and the removed set contains both losers and winners. Whether the filter helps depends entirely on the ratio between them — on the expectancy of what was deleted, not on the statistics of what remains.

The diagnostic

Standard backtest output describes the retained set only. To evaluate a filter you have to construct the removed set explicitly and measure it as if it were a strategy in its own right.

removed = all_signals − filtered_signals
E(removed) = expectancy of that set, net of cost

Then read the result:

Interpreting the removed set
E(removed)MeaningAction
Clearly negativeFilter removes real lossesKeep
Near zeroFilter removes noise and costKeep if it cuts drawdown
Clearly positiveFilter deletes profitable tradesRemove filter

Interpretation table for the method. Note the middle row: a filter that removes a set with roughly zero expectancy is still often worth keeping, because it removes commission and exposure without removing profit.

A filter that raises win rate while the removed set carries positive expectancy is the specific, common failure this paper exists to name. The trader sees a better-looking report and has quietly deleted part of their edge.

The case in our own code

The EMA flip exit is a standard construction: close the position when a fast moving average crosses back against the direction of the trade. It reads as prudent risk management — you are out when momentum turns. It ships disabled by default in our fade instruments, and the removed-set analysis is why.

A volatility fade enters against an active move, at a moment of over-extension, expecting reversion. Consider the sequence of a textbook winning fade:

  • Price extends sharply. The fade signal fires and the position is opened against the move.
  • Price continues briefly — the wide ATR-buffered stop from Paper 05 is what allows the trade to survive this.
  • Reversion begins. Price starts travelling toward the target.
  • Only after a meaningful portion of that reversion has completed does the fast EMA finally cross back.

A moving average is a lagging construction; that is not a defect, it is what an average is. So the flip arrives mid-reversion — after the trade has become profitable, before it has reached target. The exit fires and closes the position in the middle of the move it was designed to capture.

The filter is not removing losing trades. It is removing the back half of winning ones. The removed set has strongly positive expectancy, and the effect on the report is precisely the deceptive pattern described above: fewer full losses, a slightly better-looking hit rate, materially less money.

There is a deeper structural point underneath the specifics. A trend filter applied to a counter-trend strategy is a contradiction in terms. The strategy's entire thesis is that the prevailing move is over-extended and will revert. A trend filter's thesis is that the prevailing move should be respected. Running both means holding two incompatible views and letting whichever fires last determine the trade.

Filters that do work

This is not an argument against filtering. It is an argument for filtering on the right axis. The filters that survive removed-set analysis in our testing share a property: they are about conditions rather than about direction.

  • Volatility floors. Below a minimum ATR, the target sits inside the friction band from Paper 01 and the trade cannot pay for itself regardless of direction. The removed set here has reliably negative expectancy, because it consists of trades that were structurally unprofitable at entry.
  • Event exclusions. Removing scheduled high-impact releases removes trades whose outcome is driven by something the model does not observe. Removed-set expectancy is usually near zero with very high variance — the ideal profile for exclusion.
  • Structural invalidation. Not "momentum turned" but "the level this trade was based on has been broken". This is a thesis test rather than a directional overlay, and it removes trades that are genuinely wrong rather than trades that are merely slow.

The distinction between the last of those and the EMA flip is the whole lesson. Both close a trade early. One closes it because the reason for the trade no longer holds. The other closes it because a lagging indicator produced a reading. Only the first is information.

Applying this

Before adding any filter to any system, run three checks:

  • Measure the removed set. If your backtester cannot output the trades a filter deleted, that is a tooling gap worth fixing before adding filters. It is the only way to see what a filter costs.
  • Check the filter against the thesis. Does it test whether your reason for entering still holds, or does it impose a separate and possibly opposing view? The second is not risk management, it is a second strategy running inside the first.
  • Watch for win rate rising while profit falls. Treat that pattern as a red flag rather than a mixed result. It is the signature of a filter deleting winners, and it appears constantly in retail strategy development because win rate is the metric everyone optimises by feel.

We keep the EMA flip exit in the codebase and expose it as a toggle rather than deleting it, because on trend-continuation configurations — where entry and filter share a thesis — it grades differently. A filter is never good or bad on its own. It is good or bad relative to the strategy it is filtering.
