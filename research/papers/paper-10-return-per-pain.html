Return Per Unit of Pain

One instrument returned 48.66%. The other returned 6.56%. They are the same quality. This paper shows why the headline return of a strategy tells you almost nothing about how good it is, and why the number that matters is one most traders have never calculated.

A seven-fold difference that isn't one

Put those two return figures side by side and the conclusion looks obvious. One instrument made seven and a half times more money than the other over the same window. Anyone choosing between them on the basis of the headline number chooses the first, and the second looks barely worth running.

Now divide each return by the deepest drawdown it inflicted along the way.

A: 48.66 ÷ 11.59 = 4.20
B:  6.56 ÷  1.57 = 4.18

Two decimal places apart. Over the same period, on the same instrument class, two systems with wildly different headline figures delivered essentially identical return per unit of risk taken.

The difference between them was never quality. It was volume — how hard each was being run.

Why headline return is a choice, not a result

Return is a function of two things: the quality of the edge and the intensity with which it is exploited. Position size, trade frequency, target width, leverage — all of these scale return, and all of them scale drawdown at the same time and in roughly the same proportion.

Which means headline return is largely a dial, not a discovery. Take instrument B and run it at roughly seven times the risk and you would expect to arrive somewhere near instrument A's return, and near instrument A's drawdown. Nothing about the underlying edge changed. Only the volume knob moved.

This is why return-only comparisons are close to worthless, and why the marketing of trading products relies on them so heavily. Any strategy can be made to display an impressive return by turning up the risk. The number is trivially manufacturable, and it is the first thing every sales page shows.

The asymmetry that makes drawdown the real constraint

If return per unit of drawdown is equal, choosing between the two instruments comes down to which drawdown you can actually survive — and drawdown has a mathematical property that makes this less symmetric than it looks.

Recovering from a loss requires a larger gain than the loss itself, and the gap widens non-linearly:

Gain required to recover a given drawdown
DrawdownGain to recover
1.57%1.60%
11.59%13.11%
25%33.3%
50%100%
75%300%

At the top of that table the penalty is negligible. Instrument B's 1.57% drawdown needs 1.60% to recover — three basis points of friction. Instrument A's 11.59% needs 13.11%, a real but manageable gap. Push the same edge to a 50% drawdown by turning the risk dial further and recovery requires doubling the account, which is a fundamentally different proposition on identical underlying quality.

So the risk dial is not linear in consequence even when it is linear in ratio. Return per unit of drawdown stays constant as you scale up. Survivability does not.

The constraint nobody measures

There is a second constraint, and it is the one that ends most trading careers rather than the arithmetic.

A backtest experiences an 11.59% drawdown as a dip in a chart, visible in full context, with the recovery already drawn to the right of it. An operator experiences it as several weeks or months of losing money with no information about whether it ends. Those are not the same event, and the second one is where systems get abandoned — typically near the bottom, which converts a temporary drawdown into a permanent loss.

This is why the honest comparison between two equal-ratio instruments is not "which returns more" but a question about the operator:

  • What drawdown have you actually sat through before without intervening, changing settings, or turning the system off? Not what you believe you could tolerate — what you have demonstrably tolerated. Most people discover their real number is roughly half their estimated one.
  • Is the capital genuinely long-term? Money that might be needed in six months cannot ride out a drawdown that takes nine to recover, regardless of the ratio.
  • Are you automated or manual? An automated system does not lose faith. The behavioural constraint applies to the operator, and if the operator cannot intervene, the constraint is weaker.

Answer those first, then pick the instrument whose drawdown profile fits the answer. Choosing the higher-return instrument and hoping to tolerate its drawdown is the standard sequence and it is backwards.

The caveat on our own numbers

Both figures in this paper come from backtests, and drawdown is the statistic that degrades most severely out of sample. Paper 08 sets out why: characterising tail behaviour needs far more trades than establishing that an edge exists, and neither instrument has been tested at that depth.

The practical implication is uncomfortable and worth stating plainly. Your realised maximum drawdown will very likely exceed the backtested figure, because the backtest sampled a finite set of conditions and the future will supply conditions it did not contain. A reasonable working assumption is to plan for something meaningfully worse than the published number — and to select your instrument on that inflated figure rather than the one in the report.

Which sharpens the conclusion rather than softening it. If two instruments deliver identical return per unit of pain, and the pain figure is the least reliable number in the report, the correct default is the one that runs cooler. You can always add leverage to a system you understand. You cannot retroactively survive a drawdown that removed you from the market.
