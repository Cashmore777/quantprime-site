What 1.99 Actually Means

Our profit factor is 1.995 over 279 trades. That number is real, it is in-sample, and it is far less precise than three decimal places suggest. This paper puts an honest interval around our own headline figure — and hands you the arithmetic to do the same to anyone else's.

Three decimal places is a lie

Every backtest report in this industry prints its statistics to three decimal places. It looks like precision. It is formatting.

A profit factor is an estimate drawn from a sample. Like any estimate, it has an uncertainty attached, and the uncertainty is a function of how many trades produced it. Publishing 1.995 without publishing 279 alongside it is close to meaningless, and publishing it without an interval invites a reader to believe something the data cannot support.

So here is our own number, treated the way we would treat someone else's.

The interval on the win rate

Start with the component we can bound cleanly. A win rate is a proportion, and the standard error of a proportion is:

SE = √( p(1−p) / n )

With p = 0.72 and n = 279, that gives a standard error of about 2.7 percentage points. The 95% interval is roughly ±5.3 points:

Win rate, 279 trades — 95% confidence interval
QuantityValue
Observed win rate72.0%
Standard error2.69 pp
95% lower bound66.7%
95% upper bound77.3%

Now push that through to profit factor. Holding the win-to-loss size ratio fixed at whatever produces 1.995 at a 72% hit rate, the same interval on the win rate maps to:

Implied profit factor across the win-rate interval
Win rateImplied PFReading
66.7%1.55Solid

Derived by holding the average win-to-loss ratio constant and varying only the hit rate across its confidence interval. The true interval is wider than this, because the win-to-loss ratio is itself estimated from the same 279 trades and carries its own uncertainty. Treat 1.55–2.64 as a floor on the imprecision, not a bound on it.

The honest statement is not "profit factor 1.995". It is "somewhere around 1.6 to 2.6, probably, on this data."

The in-sample discount

Everything above assumes the parameters were fixed before the data was seen. They were not. Ours were selected by testing on this data — timeframe, ATR multiplier, band thresholds, session windows. That is normal and unavoidable in strategy development, and it means the reported figure is biased upward by a second mechanism entirely separate from sampling error.

The mechanism is multiple comparisons. If you evaluate twenty parameter combinations and report the best one, you have not found the best strategy. You have found the combination that best fits the noise in your sample, and its measured performance includes that fit. The more configurations tested, the larger the inflation — and almost nobody counts how many they tried.

The formal treatment of this is well established in the quantitative literature, under names like the deflated Sharpe ratio, which explicitly adjusts a reported performance statistic for the number of trials that produced it. The intuition is worth carrying even without the machinery: a backtest statistic is the maximum of a set of noisy estimates, and the maximum of a noisy set is biased high.

Practical consequence for reading any published result, ours included:

  • In-sample results are an upper bound. Not an estimate — a ceiling. Live performance below the backtest is the expected outcome, not evidence of failure.
  • Expect meaningful degradation. A 30–50% haircut on the edge, in the absence of walk-forward validation, is a reasonable working assumption. A 1.995 in-sample profit factor that lands near 1.4 live has behaved normally.
  • Ask how many configurations were tested. If the answer is not disclosed, the number is unknowable and the discount should be larger, not smaller.

How many trades do you actually need?

Sample size requirements scale with what you are trying to establish, and the difference between the tiers is larger than most traders assume:

Approximate sample requirements by claim
ClaimTradesWhat you get
Direction of edge~100Is it plausibly positive
Useful confidence300–500Interval narrow enough to act on
Tail behaviour1,000+Worst-case and drawdown character
Regime robustnessMulti-yearSurvives conditions not in sample

Approximate guidance. Requirements rise as win rate moves toward the extremes and as the payoff distribution becomes more tail-dependent — a strategy whose profits come from rare large winners needs far more trades to characterise than one with a dense outcome distribution.

At 279 trades, our figure sits in the first tier and just short of the second. It supports the claim that the edge is real and positive. It does not support a precise estimate of its magnitude, and it says almost nothing about tail behaviour — which is precisely why the drawdown figures in Paper 10 deserve more scepticism than the return figures beside them.

Why publish this about our own number

Because the alternative is worse for everyone involved. A member who deploys on the strength of 1.995 and sees 1.4 concludes they were misled. A member who deployed knowing the honest interval was 1.55–2.64 before in-sample discounting sees 1.4 and recognises it as within expectations.

The second member stays. The first one doesn't, and they're right not to.

There is also a straightforward filter available to anyone reading marketing material in this industry. Ask for three numbers: the trade count, the test window, and whether the results are in-sample. Any one of them missing tells you something. All three missing tells you everything.
